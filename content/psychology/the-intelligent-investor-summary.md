The Intelligent Investor is the book that defined value investing, and it is still the best guide to the one question most investors skip: what am I paying, and is it worth it? Benjamin Graham wrote it for ordinary investors rather than professionals, and its central ideas, Mr. Market and the margin of safety, have outlasted every market fashion since. It is long and parts of it are dated, but no other book will change how you think about price and risk as much.

| At a glance | The Intelligent Investor |
|---|---|
| Author | Benjamin Graham |
| First published | 1949, last revised by Graham in 1973 |
| Length | Over 600 pages in editions with Jason Zweig's commentary |
| Best for | Long-term investors who want a framework for valuing what they buy |
| Skip if | You want a quick read or a trading method |

Warren Buffett read it at 19, went on to study under Graham, and has called it the best book about investing ever written. Editions since 2003 add a commentary after each chapter by Wall Street Journal columnist Jason Zweig, which updates Graham's examples. Read that edition.

## The book in five ideas

### 1. Investing and speculating are different activities

For Graham, an investment is something you have analysed, that protects your capital, and that offers a reasonable return. Anything else is speculation. He did not say speculation was wrong, only that you should know when you are doing it, keep it separate and keep it small. Most losses come from people speculating while believing they are investing.

### 2. Mr. Market is your servant, not your guide

Graham's most famous image is a business partner called Mr. Market who turns up every day offering to buy your share of the business or sell you his. Some days he is euphoric and asks a ridiculous price; other days he is despairing and will sell for almost nothing. You are free to ignore him. His moods are an opportunity when they give you a good price, and noise the rest of the time. The mistake is letting his mood become yours.

### 3. Always demand a margin of safety

Graham's final chapter distils the book into three words: margin of safety. Buy only when the price is comfortably below what you believe the investment is worth, so that if your estimate is wrong, or bad luck strikes, you are still protected. The margin is what turns a reasonable guess into a sound investment.

### 4. Choose your role: defensive or enterprising

Graham splits investors by how much effort they will put in, not by how much risk they want. The defensive investor wants a sound portfolio with little work. The enterprising investor is willing to research individual opportunities in depth. Both can do well. The danger is being defensive in effort but enterprising in ambition.

### 5. Split between bonds and shares, and rebalance

Graham's defensive investor never holds less than 25% or more than 75% in shares, with the rest in high-grade bonds, and treats 50/50 as the natural default. Moving towards the bond end when shares look expensive, and back when they look cheap, builds a quiet discipline: you trim what has risen and add to what has fallen.

## The defensive investor's checklist

Graham gives the defensive investor seven tests for choosing individual shares. They are strict on purpose.

| Test | Graham's standard |
|---|---|
| Size | A large, established company |
| Financial strength | Current assets at least twice current liabilities; long-term debt no more than working capital |
| Earnings stability | Positive earnings in each of the past 10 years |
| Dividend record | Uninterrupted dividends for at least 20 years |
| Earnings growth | Earnings per share up by at least a third over 10 years, using three-year averages |
| Moderate price | No more than 15 times average earnings of the past three years |
| Moderate price to assets | No more than 1.5 times book value, or price-to-earnings times price-to-book no higher than 22.5 |

Very few companies pass every test today, partly because modern businesses carry fewer physical assets, so the book-value test is the hardest to meet. The list still works as a set of questions, even when you relax the exact numbers.

## What it means if you invest for income

### A dividend record is a quality test, not a yield target

Graham asked for 20 years of unbroken dividends. That is not about income at all. A company that has paid through two decades of recessions and crises has shown that its earnings are real and that its management takes the payout seriously. A high yield on its own tells you something different: often that the market doubts the dividend will last. Graham would have you look at the record before the rate.

### An option premium is not a margin of safety

Graham's protection comes from the price you pay for the shares. A covered call premium is a small payment for giving up upside. In [our worked example](/options/how-a-covered-call-works), $1.20 on a $50 share lowers the breakeven by 2.4%, which is nowhere near the cushion Graham had in mind. Sell calls on shares you bought with a margin of safety; do not sell calls to create one. You can check the numbers for any trade with the [covered call calculator](/tools/covered-call-calculator).

### Option sellers are trading with Mr. Market

Option premiums are prices Mr. Market sets, and they swing with his mood. When he is frightened, premiums rise; when he is calm, they shrink. That makes Graham's framework surprisingly useful for option income. Selling a put at a strike where you would gladly own the shares is a way of naming your price and being paid while you wait for Mr. Market to offer it. Selling a call when he is euphoric is a way of charging him for his optimism. The discipline Graham would insist on is the same as for shares: decide what the underlying business is worth first, and let the premium be a bonus, not the reason for the trade.

### Know whether you are investing or speculating

By Graham's definition, buying short-dated options to bet on a price move is speculation. Selling covered calls against shares you would happily hold anyway, sized sensibly, can sit within an investment approach. The test is whether you would still be comfortable owning the position if the trade went against you, and whether you have done the analysis to justify that comfort.

### Bonds are part of the plan

Graham's defensive investor is never fully in shares. For income investors that matters: high-grade bonds pay a dependable income of their own, steady the portfolio when shares fall, and provide the cash to buy when Mr. Market is selling cheaply. An income portfolio made only of high-yield shares and option premiums has none of that ballast.

## Where the book falls short

- **The examples are old.** Graham's companies and figures are from the 1960s and early 1970s. Zweig's commentary fixes most of this, which is why that edition is worth having.
- **The share-picking tests are hard to meet now.** Asset-light businesses rarely pass the book-value test, and applying the list rigidly would exclude most of today's market.
- **It is dense.** The chapters on bonds, convertibles and investment funds are heavy going. If you are short of time, read the introduction and chapters 1, 4, 5, 8, 14 and 20.
- **It is about US markets.** The principles apply anywhere, but the tax and product detail is American.

## Who should read it

Read it if you buy individual shares, or if you have ever bought something mainly because the price was going up. The chapters on Mr. Market and the margin of safety are worth the price of the book alone. If you mainly use index funds, the asset allocation chapters still apply, but you can skip most of the share-picking material. For the behavioural side of the same lessons, read Morgan Housel's [The Psychology of Money](/psychology/the-psychology-of-money-summary) alongside it.

## Common questions

### What is the main lesson of The Intelligent Investor?

Always buy with a margin of safety, a price comfortably below what the investment is worth, and treat the market's daily prices as offers you can take or ignore, not as instructions.

### Is The Intelligent Investor still relevant?

The principles are. The examples and some of the numerical tests are dated, which is why the edition with Jason Zweig's commentary is the one to read.

### Which chapters matter most?

Chapter 8, on Mr. Market and market fluctuations, and chapter 20, on the margin of safety. Chapter 14 sets out the defensive investor's stock selection tests.
